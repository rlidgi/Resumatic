A generic resume guide assumes you have a straightforward career path — steady progression in the same field, relevant job titles, and years of directly applicable experience. But what if that's not your situation?

Whether you're switching careers entirely, writing your first professional resume, or condensing 20+ years of executive experience into a compelling document, you need a different approach.

Here's how to write a resume that works for your specific situation.

Part 1: Resume for Career Changers

The Challenge

You have experience, skills, and accomplishments — but your job titles and industry don't match the role you want. A standard reverse-chronological resume makes it look like you're applying for the wrong job.

The key is reframing your experience around transferable skills and demonstrating why your background is an advantage, not a detour.

How to Structure a Career Change Resume

Use a combination format that leads with a skills-focused summary before your work history:

  1. Contact Information
  2. Professional Summary — this is critical; it bridges your old career to your new target
  3. Core Competencies — highlight transferable skills that match the target role
  4. Work Experience — reverse-chronological, but with bullets rewritten to emphasize transferable achievements
  5. Education & Certifications — include any new credentials for the target field
  6. Skills

Write a Bridge Summary

Your summary needs to connect where you've been to where you're going. Don't apologize for the change — position it as a strength.

Example — Teacher transitioning to Corporate Training:

"Educator with 8 years of experience designing and delivering curriculum for diverse audiences of 30-150 learners. Created 40+ training modules, managed program budgets of $75K, and achieved 94% participant satisfaction scores. Transitioning to corporate learning and development, bringing expertise in instructional design, needs assessment, and measurable learning outcomes."

Example — Sales professional transitioning to Product Management:

"Sales leader with 6 years closing enterprise SaaS deals ($50K-$500K ACV) and a deep understanding of buyer pain points, competitive positioning, and product-market fit. Led voice-of-customer initiatives that directly informed 3 major product releases. Pursuing product management to leverage frontline market insight and cross-functional collaboration skills."

Rewrite Your Bullet Points

The same accomplishment can be framed differently depending on the target role:

Original (for sales resume):

"Exceeded annual quota by 135%, closing $2.8M in new business"

Reframed (for product management resume):

"Conducted 200+ discovery calls with enterprise buyers, identifying 3 recurring unmet needs that informed the product roadmap and led to 2 new features generating $1.2M in first-year revenue"

Same experience, different emphasis. Focus on the skills the new role requires.

Add Relevant Credentials

If you're changing fields, new certifications show commitment and foundational knowledge:

  • To product management: Product School certification, Google Analytics
  • To data analysis: Google Data Analytics Certificate, SQL certification
  • To UX design: Google UX Design Certificate, portfolio projects
  • To project management: PMP, Scrum Master certification
  • To marketing: HubSpot certifications, Google Ads certification

Even completing an online course shows initiative. List it under Certifications or Education.

Career Change Resume Mistakes

  • Don't lead with your old job title in the summary. Lead with the skills and outcomes relevant to the new role.
  • Don't include irrelevant experience in detail. Early career roles that have nothing to do with either your old or new field can be listed as title + company + dates only.
  • Don't use a functional format. It raises red flags with recruiters and ATS. A combination format achieves the same goal without the downsides.

Part 2: Entry-Level Resume

The Challenge

You don't have much (or any) professional experience. Every job posting seems to require 3-5 years of experience you don't have. Your resume feels empty compared to people who've been working for years.

The strategy: leverage everything you do have — education, projects, internships, volunteer work, and transferable skills — and present it with the same impact-driven approach used by experienced professionals.

How to Structure an Entry-Level Resume

  1. Contact Information
  2. Professional Summary or Objective — for entry-level, an objective statement works well
  3. Education — move this above Work Experience since it's likely your strongest section
  4. Relevant Experience — internships, part-time work, volunteer roles
  5. Projects — academic or personal projects that demonstrate relevant skills
  6. Skills
  7. Activities & Leadership (optional)

Write an Objective (Not a Summary)

Entry-level candidates often don't have enough experience for a meaningful summary. An objective statement works better — it tells the employer what you're looking for and what you bring.

Formula:

[Recent graduate / Aspiring (role)] with [relevant background]. [Key skill or achievement]. Seeking [type of role] where I can [value you'll bring].

Example — Computer Science graduate:

"Recent computer science graduate from UC Berkeley with hands-on experience in full-stack development using Python, React, and PostgreSQL. Built 3 applications as part of capstone projects, including a real-time inventory management system used by a local nonprofit. Seeking a software engineering role where I can apply my technical skills and passion for building user-focused products."

Example — Marketing graduate:

"Marketing graduate with internship experience in social media management and content creation. Grew a campus organization's Instagram from 450 to 3,200 followers in one semester through data-driven content strategy. Seeking an entry-level marketing role in B2B SaaS."

Maximize Your Education Section

For entry-level, your education section can include more detail:

EDUCATION B.S. Computer Science, University of California, Berkeley | May 2026 GPA: 3.7 | Dean's List (6 semesters) Relevant Coursework: Machine Learning, Database Systems, Software Engineering, Data Structures & Algorithms Senior Capstone: Built a real-time inventory management system using React, Node.js, and PostgreSQL for a local nonprofit, reducing manual tracking time by 80%

Turn Projects into Experience

Academic and personal projects count — especially if you can show results:

PROJECTS Inventory Management System | React, Node.js, PostgreSQL - Built a full-stack web application for a local nonprofit to track 2,000+ inventory items across 3 locations - Reduced manual data entry time by 80% and eliminated duplicate records - Implemented role-based access control and automated low-stock alerts Market Analysis Dashboard | Python, Tableau - Analyzed 50,000+ rows of sales data to identify seasonal trends and customer segments - Created interactive Tableau dashboard used by marketing team to inform $200K campaign budget allocation - Presented findings to a panel of 4 industry professionals, receiving highest project grade in cohort

Entry-Level Resume Mistakes

  • Don't leave it mostly empty. If you don't have work experience, fill the space with projects, coursework, volunteer work, and skills. A full one-page resume is always better than a half-page one.
  • Don't list every class you've ever taken. Only include coursework directly relevant to the target role.
  • Don't include high school. Once you're in or through college, high school drops off.
  • Don't use an unprofessional email address. Create a simple firstname.lastname@gmail.com if needed.
  • Don't skip quantification. Even academic projects have numbers: users served, data points analyzed, team members, timeline.

Part 3: Executive Resume

The Challenge

You have too much experience, not too little. Condensing 15-25+ years of leadership, strategy, and organizational impact into a 2-page resume requires ruthless prioritization. You also need to convey strategic thinking and business impact, not just tactical execution.

How to Structure an Executive Resume

  1. Contact Information
  2. Executive Summary — your leadership thesis in 3-4 sentences
  3. Core Competencies — strategic capabilities, not task-level skills
  4. Professional Experience — reverse-chronological, 3-4 most recent/relevant roles with detailed bullets
  5. Earlier Career — condensed listing (title + company + dates)
  6. Education
  7. Board Memberships / Affiliations (optional)
  8. Publications / Speaking (optional)

Write an Executive Summary, Not a Professional Summary

At the executive level, your summary should read like a leadership positioning statement:

Example — VP of Engineering:

"Engineering executive with 18 years of experience building and scaling technology organizations from startup to IPO. Led teams of 50-200 engineers across 4 companies, delivering platforms that process $2B+ in annual transaction volume. Track record of transforming engineering culture — reducing release cycles from quarterly to weekly, improving retention from 72% to 94%, and building diversity programs that increased underrepresented hiring by 180%."

Example — CFO:

"Finance executive with 20 years of experience across public and private companies ($50M-$2B revenue). Led 2 successful IPOs, managed $500M+ in capital allocation, and directed M&A transactions totaling $340M. Known for building finance organizations that serve as strategic partners to the business — not just reporting functions."

Focus on Strategic Impact

Executive bullet points should show organizational and business-level outcomes:

Tactical (too low-level) Strategic (right level)
Managed a team of engineers Built the engineering organization from 12 to 85 engineers across 6 teams, establishing the technical architecture that supports $400M ARR
Improved quarterly revenue Drove revenue growth from $28M to $92M over 3 years through market expansion, pricing optimization, and enterprise sales motion
Reduced costs Led a company-wide operational efficiency initiative that reduced annual operating costs by $12M while maintaining NPS above 70

Two Pages Is Standard (and Expected)

For executives, a two-page resume is normal and expected. You need the space to convey the scope of your experience. But every line must earn its place:

  • Page 1: Summary, core competencies, and your most recent 1-2 roles with detailed achievements
  • Page 2: Earlier roles (less detail), education, board memberships, and other credentials

Condense Early Career

Roles from 15+ years ago don't need full bullet points. List them as:

EARLIER CAREER Director of Engineering, TechStartup Inc. | 2008 - 2012 Senior Software Engineer, BigCorp | 2004 - 2008 Software Engineer, FirstJob LLC | 2001 - 2004

Executive Resume Mistakes

  • Don't list every job you've ever held. Focus on the last 15 years in detail.
  • Don't write at a tactical level. Recruiters hiring VPs and C-suite don't care that you "created PowerPoint presentations." They care about organizational transformation and P&L impact.
  • Don't skip metrics. Revenue, growth percentages, team sizes, market expansion, cost reduction — executives are expected to lead with numbers.
  • Don't use a generic template. At the executive level, clean and professional formatting matters. No graphics, no gimmicks — but polished, consistent, and authoritative.
